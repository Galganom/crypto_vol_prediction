Abstract
We propose News Event Decay (NED), a neural architecture for high-frequency
cryptocurrency volatility forecasting that represents news through article-dependent
magnitudes and decay rates. Pretrained CryptoBERT embeddings determine these
parameters, and their decaying signals form a bounded correction to a neural time￾series variance forecast. Across six expanding-window test periods from 2023 to 2025,
NED reduces Bitcoin QLIKE by 7.38% at the one-hour horizon and 10.83% at four
hours, with log-MSE reductions of 0.99% and 3.11% (p < 0.01). Ethereum QLIKE
gains are smaller and its log-MSE differences are insignificant. Bitcoin improvements
are strongest in the tails of the subsequent standardized-return distribution. In BTC
four-hour models, the article half-life has a median of 42.7 minutes, and negative￾classified articles receive larger average signal magnitudes than positive-classified arti￾cles. Macro news receives the largest mean magnitudes and longest median half-lives.
Matched experiments favor learned decay over constant aggregation, although the con￾stant kernel also improves forecasts. Sentiment with learned decay nearly matches the
full embedding, while PCA compression improves on it in this run.
Keywords: Realized volatility; Volatility forecasting; News-driven volatility; Cryptocur￾rency; Hawkes processes; Text embeddings.

1 Introduction
Cryptocurrency markets absorb information at intraday frequencies. A regulatory action
against an exchange, a macroeconomic announcement, or a disruption to trading infrastruc￾ture can coincide with a sharp change in volatility, while routine commentary may carry
little new information. For short-horizon forecasting, the operational question is how to
convert a stream of irregularly arriving text into a useful component of a volatility forecast.
This requires attention both to what an article says and to how its relevance changes after
publication.
Two research traditions motivate our approach. The first, rooted in the point-process
literature, represents events as discrete impulses whose effects accumulate and dissipate
over time. Hawkes processes (Hawkes, 1971) and their applications in finance (Bacry et al.,
2015) provide a natural vocabulary for these dynamics. Neural extensions allow richer de￾pendence on event history (Mei and Eisner, 2017; Zuo et al., 2020). The second tradition
extracts information from financial text using sentiment classifiers and contextual represen￾tations, including FinBERT (Araci, 2019), CryptoBERT (Kulakowski and Frasincar, 2023),
and financial word embeddings (Rahimikia et al., 2023). Such representations complement
the evidence that sentiment and attention can contribute to volatility forecasting (Audrino
et al., 2020).
These perspectives suggest that text representation and temporal aggregation should
be considered together. A fixed-window average does not distinguish an article published
moments ago from one near the beginning of the window. A common discount factor distin￾guishes their ages but assigns the same persistence to every article. Two articles published
in the same hour, one describing an enforcement action and another offering routine market
commentary, may warrant different magnitudes and durations of forecast adjustment. We
ask whether these two quantities can be learned jointly, at the article level, from the content
of the news.
We propose News Event Decay (NED), a neural architecture that maps each article’s
pretrained CryptoBERT embedding into a positive magnitude sk and a decay rate λk. The
news branch sums the signals sk exp(−λk∆tk) over a fixed lookback window and maps their
aggregate into a bounded correction to a time-series forecast. The forecast is multiplicative
in realized variance and additive in its logarithm. Although the aggregation is inspired
by event-decay kernels, NED is a conditional forecasting model: it does not estimate an
event-arrival intensity or a self-exciting point process.
The separation between the time-series baseline and the news branch serves two purposes.
It asks news to account for forecast information beyond a baseline already trained on price
2
and volume history, and it retains an explicit description of how each article enters the
aggregate signal. The final forecast also depends on a fitted residual loading, an intercept,
and clipping. Consequently, article magnitudes are interpretable within the fitted correction,
rather than as standalone estimates of causal volatility shocks.
We evaluate one-hour and four-hour realized-variance forecasts on 15-minute Bitcoin and
Ethereum bars. Six expanding-window test periods cover January 2023 through December
2025. The price histories used for training begin in 2017 for Bitcoin and 2018 for Ethereum.
CoinDesk provides the news corpus; a deterministic tag and title filter removes common
price recaps before articles are aligned to forecast origins. We compare NED with HAR,
EGARCH, an EGARCH+NED hybrid, and a TS-only model that is exactly the first-phase
time-series component of NED.
The main results establish a forecast benefit for Bitcoin. Relative to TS-only, NED
reduces QLIKE by 7.38% at one hour and 10.83% at four hours, with corresponding log￾MSE reductions of 0.99% and 3.11%. The pooled loss differences are significant at the
1% level under the Diebold–Mariano test with the small-sample correction of Harvey et al.
(1997). Ethereum gains are smaller: QLIKE declines by 1.32% and 2.04%, while log-MSE
differences are not significant. The contrast between assets limits the scope of a general
claim about the forecasting value of this news source.
A second finding concerns where the Bitcoin gains arise. Sorting four-hour forecasts
by the subsequent return scaled by TS-only volatility reveals larger improvements in both
return tails. In the central deciles, QLIKE improves modestly while log-MSE can deteriorate.
Similarly, adding the news branch to EGARCH improves log-MSE but worsens QLIKE. News
augmentation therefore changes the distribution of forecast errors in ways that a single
accuracy measure does not fully describe.
A third set of results examines what the article parameters represent. Across the BTC
four-hour test sample, the median estimated half-life is 42.7 minutes, and larger internal
signals tend to decay more slowly. The case study, event-category comparison, and senti￾ment analysis describe this heterogeneity from complementary perspectives. Macro news
receives the largest mean magnitude and longest median half-life among the twelve cate￾gories. Negative-classified articles receive larger average magnitudes than positive-classified
articles, but sentiment ambiguity does not produce a stable separation. These are properties
of the learned predictive representation rather than identified structural responses.
Finally, matched experiments examine the design choices behind the news branch. A
constant kernel already improves QLIKE, and learned decay provides additional gains, with
linear decay performing best in the kernel comparison. Sentiment with learned decay nearly
matches the full-dimensional embedding, while a 256-component PCA representation im￾3
proves on it in this run. These comparisons, together with lookback sensitivity, distinguish
the usefulness of the news mechanism from the optimality of the main specification. All
variants share the same fitted time-series baseline within each expanding window.
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the data, architecture,
training procedure, baselines, and evaluation protocol. Section 3 presents the main results
and the Z-score analysis. Section 4 studies article persistence, news categories, and sentiment.
Section 5 compares alternative decay functions and news representations, with lookback
sensitivity reported in the Appendix. Section 6 concludes.

2.1 Data and preprocessing

and bars without a closing price are dropped. After feature construction and alignment with

Ethereum. The out-of-sample analysis covers six half-year test periods from January 2023

From the 15-minute OHLCV bars, we engineer twelve features that capture multi-scale

2.1.3 News corpus and reactive filtering
We use CoinDesk news records with article text and UTC publication timestamps. Before
January 2026, the processed corpus contains 25,447 BTC-labelled records and 10,277 ETH￾labelled records. These are asset-specific observations: an article associated with both assets
5
can appear in both samples. After the recap filter, 19,249 BTC and 8,735 ETH records
remain. The underlying file also contains early-2026 articles; the publication-time cutoff
prevents those later records from entering any forecast in the 2023–2025 test sample.
A key preprocessing step addresses reactive news contamination: articles that describe a
price movement already in progress rather than report a new information event. We remove
articles carrying the editorial tags price-news, Markets News, Market Wrap, Bitcoin Price,
Technical Analysis, First Mover, Bitcoin Long & Short, Crypto Long & Short, Markets
Daily, Asia Pacific, or Trading. A small title-based augmentation matches “market wrap”,
Bitcoin/BTC followed by a dollar sign at the beginning of a headline, and the First Mover
Asia/Americas/Europe series. The filter is deterministic and does not use future returns.
Filtering reduces the direct use of price-recap language, but it is not a guarantee that ev￾ery retained article represents an exogenous shock. An article reporting a market movement
can still survive when its tags and title do not match the rules. This distinction matters for
the interpretation of the case study and category results below.
For each forecast, we retrieve retained articles in the preceding twelve hours, with publi￾cation times strictly earlier than the stored bar timestamp. The timestamp is the first raw
observation in the resampled bar, generally its left-hand endpoint. Since the price features
use the completed current bar, forecasts are interpreted as end-of-bar forecasts with this
earlier news cutoff. The news age used by the model is measured from the stored timestamp.
These timing conventions are applied consistently to the main model and its ablations.
2.1.4 CryptoBERT embeddings
Each article is represented by a 768-dimensional vector from the pretrained ElKulako/
cryptobert checkpoint (Kulakowski and Frasincar, 2023). The encoder processes the stored
article text, truncated to at most 512 tokens. We average its final hidden states over the
positions enabled by the attention mask and normalize the resulting vector to unit Euclidean
length. Embeddings are precomputed and held fixed during volatility-model training.
The main model uses the complete 768-dimensional representation, followed by a learned
LayerNorm at the input to the news head. No PCA is applied in the primary specification. A
256-dimensional representation is introduced only in the news-representation ablation, where
PCA is fitted separately on training-period retained articles in each window. CryptoBERT’s
cryptocurrency-oriented representation offers a way to encode event descriptions beyond
a fixed sentiment category. Whether that additional information improves forecasts is an
empirical question addressed in Section 5.2.

2.2.1 Time-series branch
The time-series branch maps the twelve standardized features to a positive variance forecast
through a feedforward network with widths 12 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 1. Hidden layers
use ReLU activations, the first hidden layer is followed by dropout with probability 0.2,
and the output uses Softplus. This can be viewed as a nonlinear extension of the multi-scale
HAR representation: variance components enter alongside return, range, volume, and higher￾moment features, allowing their interactions to be learned rather than imposed linearly.

2.3.1 Expanding window evaluation
Each model is trained on all observations before a six-month validation period and evalu￾ated on the subsequent half-year. The training history expands monotonically, and model
selection uses validation loss only. The six test periods begin in January 2023, July 2023,
January 2024, July 2024, January 2025, and July 2025. The total test sample is 104,570
BTC bars and 104,641 ETH bars at each horizon.
Two implementation conventions qualify this chronological design. Validation and test
upper bounds include 00:00 UTC on their final date, omitting the remaining bars on that
date. Training labels are not purged when their forward returns extend across the start of
validation. These conventions are held fixed across comparisons; sensitivity to an embargo
and full end-of-day bounds remains a limitation of the experimental design. The ablations
reuse each window’s selected P1 state and retrain the alternative P2 branches on the same
partitions.

Table 3 reports forecast performance at both horizons for Bitcoin and Ethereum. We
begin with the models that do not use news. The adapted HAR model has negative mean￾window R2 at both horizons for both assets. EGARCH improves on this raw-variance HAR
specification, and the neural TS-only model further lowers log-MSE. At the BTC four-hour
14
horizon, TS-only attains R2 = 0.419, compared with 0.191 for EGARCH. The nonlinear
model therefore provides a demanding baseline against which to assess the incremental value
of a news correction.
Adding NED produces significant BTC gains on both forecast losses. At four hours,
QLIKE falls from 0.63815 to 0.56902, a reduction of 10.83%, while log-MSE falls from 0.80974
to 0.78453, a reduction of 3.11%. At one hour, the respective improvements are 7.38% and
0.99%. All four aggregate BTC comparisons have p < 0.001 under the corrected DM test.
Improvements in R2 are more modest, from 0.41674 to 0.42278 at one hour and from 0.41932
to 0.43759 at four hours. The stronger QLIKE response indicates that the news correction
affects the calibration of variance levels beyond its smaller change in average squared log
error.
Ethereum provides a less uniform result. QLIKE improves by 1.32% at one hour and
2.04% at four hours, with both aggregate comparisons significant at the 1% level. Log-MSE
is almost unchanged: it falls by about 0.015% at one hour and rises by 0.128% at four hours,
with neither difference statistically significant. Thus the BTC finding extends to ETH under
QLIKE, but not under both losses.
Comparing NED with EGARCH+NED clarifies the role of the baseline. The hybrid
lowers EGARCH log-MSE in all four asset–horizon settings. For BTC four-hour forecasts,
log-MSE falls from 1.12726 to 0.92985 and R2
rises from 0.19067 to 0.33382. These propor￾tional MSE gains are larger than those obtained by correcting the stronger neural baseline.
However, EGARCH+NED worsens QLIKE in every setting: BTC four-hour QLIKE rises
from 0.57616 to 0.72304. The forecast value of the correction is therefore loss-dependent,
rather than an improvement that applies to every measure and every baseline.
Standalone EGARCH remains the lowest-QLIKE model for BTC one hour and for both
ETH horizons. NED has the lowest QLIKE for BTC four hours and the lowest log-MSE in
three of the four settings; TS-only has slightly lower log-MSE for ETH four hours. These
rankings underscore the importance of evaluating more than one loss function. A model
trained on robust log errors need not minimize variance-ratio loss, even when it improves on
an identical time-series checkpoint.
Per-window results in Tables 10 and 11 show that BTC gains are positive in all six
windows at both horizons and under both losses. The largest four-hour QLIKE gain, 24.62%,
occurs in the first half of 2024. ETH improves in QLIKE over the first four windows, but the
gain reverses in both 2025 windows: QLIKE worsens by 3.34% and 3.17% at one hour, and by
4.73% and 4.85% at four hours. The aggregate ETH finding consequently masks economically
relevant variation across periods. Unequal news coverage may be one explanation, but these
comparisons do not identify the cause of the asset difference.

4 Economic Interpretation
The NED framework represents each article through two quantities: an impact magnitude
sk and a decay rate λk. These parameters provide a way to examine which kinds of text the
model associates with large and persistent adjustments to a volatility forecast. Their inter￾pretation is conditional on the residual loading a, the intercept b, and the clipping operation
in Equation (3). In particular, sk is an internal signal magnitude; the initial contribution to
the unclipped log-variance correction is ask. The parameters describe predictive associations
rather than causal responses to externally identified news shocks.
We organize the analysis around three questions: how long does news move volatility
(Section 4.1), how does news content shape the impact (Section 4.2), and what dimension of
the text representation does the model use (Section 4.3)? The analysis uses the BTC 4-hour
model and the 7,845 retained articles that enter at least one of its out-of-sample forecasts
across the six expanding windows. Each article contributes one parameter pair estimated by
the corresponding window’s fitted model.

5 Robustness and sensitivity analysis
The main specification combines a rich article representation with a particular temporal
aggregation rule. This section asks how forecast accuracy changes when those components
are varied. We focus on BTC four-hour forecasts and use all six expanding windows. Each
experiment loads the same fitted first-phase TS-only model used in the main results, freezes
its weights, and estimates a new second-phase news correction. The learning rate, loss,
batch size, epoch limit, early stopping rule, and gradient clipping match the main NED
specification. Thus, a difference between variants reflects the fitted news correction rather
than a different first-phase baseline.
22
We refit the full-dimensional exponential specification along with the alternatives. This
provides a common reference trained with the same numerical implementation and shuffled
batch sequence as the ablation models. It need not reproduce the main-results checkpoint
exactly, since the latter uses a different batch-generation path. The experiments use one fixed
training seed and report arithmetic means across the six test windows. Their differences are
descriptive comparisons rather than estimates averaged over training seeds.

6 Conclusion
This paper has asked whether irregularly arriving cryptocurrency news can be represented by
article-specific magnitudes and temporal decay profiles that improve short-horizon volatility
forecasts. NED maps a fixed text embedding into these two quantities, sums their decaying
signals, and learns a bounded residual correction to a time-series variance forecast. The
staged training procedure preserves a common no-news baseline and makes the incremental
forecast comparison explicit.
Across six expanding-window test periods, the main model improves Bitcoin QLIKE by
7.38% at one hour and 10.83% at four hours, alongside smaller but significant reductions in
squared log error. The gains are present in every BTC test window and are strongest in the
tails of the subsequent return distribution. For Ethereum, average QLIKE improvements
are smaller, reverse in the 2025 windows, and are not accompanied by significant log-MSE
improvements. The contrast illustrates that the value of a news correction depends on the
asset, period, and evaluation loss.
The same qualification applies to the choice of baseline. Adding a news residual to
EGARCH improves log-MSE more than adding it to the stronger neural baseline, but wors￾ens QLIKE. NED has the lowest QLIKE among the evaluated models for BTC four-hour
forecasts; standalone EGARCH retains that position in the other three asset–horizon set￾tings. These results favor evaluating news augmentation through several complementary
measures rather than treating it as a uniform improvement in forecast quality.
The ablations qualify a simple claim that either temporal decay or encoder richness is
sufficient to explain the gains. A constant article kernel already reduces QLIKE by 7.16%;
learned decay improves further, with linear decay producing the lowest losses in the kernel
comparison. Sentiment probabilities combined with learned decay nearly match the full￾dimensional CryptoBERT reference, and the 256-component PCA representation improves
on it in this run. These results support compact text representations and explicit tempo￾ral weighting as useful design choices, while showing that the exponential, full-dimensional
specification is not uniquely favored by the forecast comparison. Forecast improvements also
26
persist with lookbacks of six, twelve, and twenty-four hours, with six hours giving the lowest
mean losses in the lookback comparison.
The learned parameters also offer a useful description of the model’s response to text.
Most BTC four-hour article signals have short half-lives, with a median of 42.7 minutes,
while a smaller group persists for several hours. Larger signals tend to decay more slowly,
and negative sentiment is associated with larger magnitudes than positive sentiment. Macro
news has the largest mean magnitude and longest median half-life among the event categories.
The March 2024 episode adds context to these patterns. Their interpretation remains condi￾tional on the residual loading and calibration, and on the limits of automated filtering and
classification.
Several limitations suggest directions for further work. The news corpus comes from one
English-language publisher and has unequal coverage of the two assets. Broader sources
would help assess whether the cross-asset difference persists. Training labels overlap the
validation boundary, and the current date bounds omit most of the last day of each evalu￾ation period; an embargoed design with complete calendar endpoints would strengthen the
chronology. The price features and news cutoff also use different points within the same bar,
which should be reconciled in a deployment-oriented timing study. Finally, the single-seed
ablations do not quantify optimization variability, and the interpretable signal decomposition
does not identify causal news effects. Multiple-seed comparisons, placebo news assignments,
and an intercept-only residual control would help distinguish content-specific information
from general forecast recalibration.
The contribution of NED is to make article timing and persistence explicit parts of a
volatility forecast while retaining a compact description of the news correction. The evidence
supports that mechanism for Bitcoin in the evaluated sample and defines the conditions under
which its apparent advantages require further testing.

Sensitivity to lookback horizon
We repeat the BTC four-hour exponential-decay experiment with lookbacks of six, twelve,
and twenty-four hours, keeping the P1 baseline fixed and refitting P2 at each horizon. All
comparisons use the same six test periods and the same training objective, learning rate,
batch size, and stopping rule. This separates a change in the available news history from a
change in the time-series model.

Lookback R2 DA QLIKE MSE
6 hours 0.437 0.724 0.559 0.786
12 hours 0.434 0.723 0.571 0.790

Notes: Arithmetic means across six windows. The twelve-hour specification is the refitted exponential
reference reported in Table 8. These are separate P2 fits, not test-time truncations of a fixed twelve-hour
model.
The forecast improvement persists across all three lookbacks in Table 12. Six hours
produces the lowest mean QLIKE, 0.55898, and log-MSE, 0.78577. At twelve hours the losses
are 0.57071 and 0.78967, while extending the window to twenty-four hours gives 0.56564 and
0.78618. The six-hour and twenty-four-hour log-MSE values are particularly close. Thus the
main result is not confined to a twelve-hour news history, and a longer lookback does not
produce a monotonic improvement. These separately fitted single-seed comparisons do not
identify an optimal lookback beyond the evaluated sample; choosing a new default would
require further validation.
